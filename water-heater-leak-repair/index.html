Where is the water coming from? That question decides whether a water heater leak costs a little or a lot. Relief valves weep when they wear out or when household pressure runs high. Drain valves, especially the plastic ones found on many factory tanks, loosen and drip with age. Fittings on top of the tank corrode where copper meets steel, and flexible connectors fail at the gasket. Condensation can mimic a leak convincingly on a gas unit or in a humid space. And sometimes the tank itself has rusted through. Across Dearborn, MI, we sort out which one you have before recommending a thing.

A leak call with us starts with evidence, not guesses. The technician wipes the tank and fittings dry so fresh water shows where it starts, then checks the relief valve and its discharge line, the drain valve at the base, and every connection on top. On gas models we also inspect the burner compartment, because water there can be normal startup condensation or a sign of a tank that has failed. A relief valve that keeps releasing water triggers a pressure test: static pressure above roughly 80 psi, or a missing expansion tank on a closed system, will keep a new valve weeping. The fix follows the findings.

With the source confirmed, the fix is often simple. A new T&P valve, a brass drain valve in place of a weeping plastic one, fresh dielectric fittings or supply connectors, and an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve when pressure is the cause can all be handled on the spot. A corroded tank is different. No sealant or patch will hold, so we explain replacement honestly and help you choose the right heater. Warning Signs to Watch For

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • A puddle or damp spot on the floor around the base of the tank
  • Water stains, soft flooring, or musty smells near the heater closet
  • A weeping drain spigot on the lower front of the heater
  • Corroded pipe connections or damp flex lines above the heater
  • Dampness in the combustion area of a gas unit
  • Water dripping from the relief valve or out of the end of its discharge pipe
  • Rust-tinted hot water and signs of seepage near the base
  • The water meter moving when nothing in the house is running

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Water heater leaks are easy to misread. A drip that appears to come from the bottom may start at a fitting on top and run down the jacket, and condensation on a cold tank can look exactly like a failure. Buying the wrong part is money down the drain, and missing a failing tank can lead to a much bigger flood later. There are safety concerns, too. The relief valve protects against dangerous temperature and pressure, and capping or plugging it to stop a drip can set up a violent failure. Corroded fittings on an older tank can crack under a wrench, and electric heaters sit on 240-volt circuits that do not mix well with wet floors.

Our technicians trace leaks methodically and fix the cause, not just the drip. We start by drying the unit, let new water reveal the source, test water pressure when the relief valve is involved, and check for thermal expansion problems that make valves weep. We replace valves and fittings with durable replacements, swap plastic drain valves for brass, and add an expansion tank or pressure-reducing valve when pressure is the root problem. When the tank is the problem, we say so directly and explain replacement options, including the drain pan and leak protection that can keep the next failure from soaking your floors.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Ask about expansion control if your home has a check valve or backflow preventer
  • Make a habit of checking around the heater for moisture or corrosion every few weeks
  • Add a floor sensor or an automatic shutoff valve that closes the supply when it detects water
  • Check your water pressure; if it runs above about 80 psi, a pressure-reducing valve protects the tank and valves
  • Place the heater in a drain pan with a line to a floor drain or outdoors, especially above finished space
  • Draining sediment every year helps the bottom of the tank last longer
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years; once it is used up, the tank starts to corrode
