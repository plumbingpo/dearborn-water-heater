Not every water heater job is a straight replacement. Plenty of Dearborn, MI projects start with a finished basement or a new addition that needs its own heater. Other jobs involve moving a unit out of a cramped closet, stepping up to a bigger tank for a growing family, or switching fuels entirely. Each of those jobs involves new gas piping, new vent routes, new circuits, or new water lines, and each one has to be planned before the first pipe is cut. Dearborn Water Heater handles installations of every size, from a single point-of-use heater under a sink to a whole-house tankless system.

Sizing and placement shape most of the outcome. For a tank, we compare first-hour ratings against your household's busiest hour; for a tankless unit, we size by flow rate and winter temperature rise. Then comes placement. A gas heater needs a vent route and combustion air, a heat pump heater needs open space and a condensate drain, and a whole-house electric tankless unit usually needs a big dedicated circuit and matching panel capacity. Put a heater above a finished ceiling and it needs a drain pan; put a gas unit in a garage and code may require a raised platform. You get the trade-offs in plain terms, with no pressure.

The final stretch is where a careful installer and a fast one look nothing alike. Every gas joint is leak-tested, vent pitch and clearances follow the manufacturer's spec, the circuit and breaker match the heater, and dielectric fittings separate copper from steel. We pipe the relief valve discharge full size to the right spot, install code-required expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps, and handle the permit if needed. Finally we fill, purge, and fire the heater, check draft and carbon monoxide, and set the temperature near 120 degrees. Call (313) 540-8638 to schedule an assessment for your Dearborn home.

How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • A remodel that moves the utility space to a new spot
  • Taps at the far end of the house that take minutes to warm up
  • Hot water running out before everyone has showered
  • A water heater installed above finished rooms with no drain pan
  • Missing code items like an expansion tank, drain pan, or proper venting
  • A new jetted tub that outpaces the heater's capacity
  • A planned switch from gas to electric, or to a heat pump model
  • An addition too far from the existing heater for quick hot water

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Consider the gas side first. A new heater on an undersized line starves for fuel whenever the furnace or range is running, and a whole-house tankless unit often needs a larger line than the house already has. The vent has to be the right material, size, and pitch, with proper clearances, so exhaust leaves the home instead of drifting back into it. Electric models need their own math on breaker, wire gauge, and panel capacity. Add relief valve piping, dielectric fittings, expansion tanks, drain pans, seismic straps, and permit rules that vary from one jurisdiction to the next, and there are a lot of details to get right.

A professional installation covers the whole job, not just setting the heater. Sizing by first-hour rating or flow rate, tested gas piping, a vent routed to spec, a circuit matched to the load, and the expansion tank, pan, or straps your code requires are all included. Before we go, gas units are checked for draft and carbon monoxide. You end up with an installation that is safe, properly sized, and ready for inspection. If a smaller or simpler heater fits your needs better, you will hear it from us directly.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Glance at the pan and discharge pipe every month or two; water there means it is time to call
  • Keep flammable liquids and clutter away from gas heaters
  • A 120-degree setting protects against burns and slows mineral buildup
  • Schedule regular maintenance, including an anode rod check
  • Keep a clear path to the heater so it can be serviced and inspected easily
  • Drain a few gallons from a new tank twice a year to control sediment
  • Tankless heaters need a yearly flush to clear scale from the heat exchanger
